Fiqh: Islamic Jurisprudence

Foundations of Faith 15 Min Read By Noor Al-Huda Team

Fiqh literally means "deep understanding." In Islamic terminology, it refers to the human effort to derive legal rulings from the divine sources (Quran and Sunnah). It governs every aspect of a Muslim's life, from how to pray and fast to how to conduct business and resolve family disputes.

"So ask the people of the message if you do not know." Surah An-Nahl, 16:43

The Difference Between Sharia and Fiqh

It is important to distinguish between these two terms:

  • Sharia: The divine, unchanging path revealed by Allah. It is perfect and universal.
  • Fiqh: The human understanding of that path. It is dynamic, contextual, and can vary based on time, place, and scholarly interpretation.

Think of Sharia as the water source, and Fiqh as the different cups people use to drink from it. The water is one, but the vessels may differ in shape and size.

The Five Rulings (Al-Ahkam Al-Khamsa)

Every action in Islam falls into one of five categories:

  1. Fard/Wajib: Obligatory (e.g., Five Daily Prayers).
  2. Mandub/Mustahabb: Recommended/Sunnah (e.g., Extra prayers).
  3. Mubah: Permissible/Neutral (e.g., Eating apples).
  4. Makruh: Disliked but not sinful (e.g., Wasting water).
  5. Haram: Forbidden (e.g., Consuming alcohol).

Branches of Fiqh

Islamic jurisprudence is broadly divided into two main categories:

Fiqh al-Ibadah

Worship: Rules governing the relationship between the servant and Allah. This includes purification, prayer, fasting, Zakat, and Hajj. These rulings are generally fixed and do not change with time.

Fiqh al-Muamalat

Transactions: Rules governing relationships between people. This includes business contracts, marriage, divorce, inheritance, and criminal law. These rulings are more flexible and adapt to changing social contexts.

The Four Major Schools of Thought (Madhhabs)

Over centuries, great scholars developed systematic methodologies for deriving laws. Four Sunni schools have survived and are widely followed today:

  • Hanafi

    Founded by Imam Abu Hanifa. Known for its use of reason and analogy. Predominant in Turkey, South Asia, and Central Asia.

  • Maliki

    Founded by Imam Malik. Relies heavily on the practice of the people of Medina. Predominant in North and West Africa.

  • Shafi'i

    Founded by Imam Al-Shafi'i. Known for systematizing legal theory. Predominant in East Africa, Yemen, and Southeast Asia.

  • Hanbali

    Founded by Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal. Strictly adheres to text over opinion. Predominant in Saudi Arabia and Qatar.

Diversity as a Mercy

The existence of different schools is considered a mercy from Allah. It allows for flexibility in different contexts. For example, a ruling suitable for a desert climate might differ from one suitable for a tropical region. As long as an opinion is derived through correct methodology, it is respected. The famous scholar Imam Al-Shafi'i said: "My opinion is correct with the possibility of being wrong, and the opinion of others is wrong with the possibility of being correct."

The Prophet ﷺ said: "When a judge gives a ruling, having tried his best to decide correctly, and he is right, he will have a double reward. If he gives a ruling, having tried his best to decide correctly, and he is wrong, he will have a single reward."

— Sahih Bukhari

Conclusion

Fiqh is not meant to be a burden, but a guide to living a balanced and righteous life. By understanding the legal rulings, we ensure that our worship is accepted and our interactions are just. It connects our daily actions to our faith, turning mundane habits into acts of devotion.
